Catalog description. Computational tools for solving a broad range of microeconomic and macroeconomic models from partial and general equilibrium models to dynamic models, growth modeling, neural nets, and portfolio models.

This offering. We build models in Python, starting from a supply-and-demand market and ending with an agent-based model. Along the way you’ll work with real economic data, solve models numerically, analyze market structure and welfare, and estimate economic relationships. No programming experience is assumed.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

  1. Program. Write, test, and debug Python code that solves economic problems, and manage your work with Git and GitHub.
  2. Work with data. Load, clean, combine, summarize, and visualize economic data.
  3. Solve models numerically. Use root-finding, optimization, linear algebra, and simulation to solve economic models, including ones with no pencil-and-paper solution.
  4. Apply microeconomics computationally. Build computational versions of consumer and producer choice, market structure, game theory, and general equilibrium, and use them for comparative statics and welfare analysis.
  5. Estimate. Estimate economic relationships with regression and maximum likelihood, and distinguish prediction from causal inference.
  6. Model with agents. Build an agent-based model and compare its aggregate outcomes with an analytical model.
  7. Communicate and work responsibly. Present computational results in reproducible notebooks with clear writing, equations, and figures, and use AI assistants critically and with transparent disclosure.

How the course works

Each week has a lecture notebook on the course website, which we work through in class with live coding, and, in most weeks, an in-class lab in the second session where you practice in pairs. Most weeks also have a problem set that appears in your private GitHub repository. You work on it in your codespace, push it to GitHub, and submit the commit link in Canvas. The Setup guide walks you through all of this in Week 1, and the Setup FAQ covers common problems.

Expected effort. ECONS 431 is a 3-credit course. Plan on about 9 hours a week: about 3 in class and about 6 outside class on readings, problem sets, and the capstone project. Programming takes longer when you’re new to it; that’s normal, and it speeds up.

Assessments and grading

Component Weight Details
Problem sets (PS01–PS14) 35% Weekly; each ends with an AI usage note. Your lowest score, other than PS01, is dropped.
Midterm exam 15% Week 8, one class session: a short paper part and a computational part in your codespace. Covers Modules 1–3. No AI.
In-class labs 10% Twelve labs, done in pairs during class; your ten best count, 1% each.
Agent-based model lab 10% Week 15, individually or in pairs.
Capstone project 30% Teams of 2–3: proposal (5%), progress check-in (5%), final notebook, repository, and written summary (12%), and presentation (8%).

Problem sets combine code, which must run from top to bottom, with written answers in Markdown. Parts are checked by running your notebook; written answers, figures, and explanations are graded by hand. Automated feedback during the semester is for practice only and doesn’t set your grade.

In-class labs. The second class session of Weeks 2–7 and 9–14 is a lab: twelve in all, and your best ten count, 1% each. You work in pairs on a short notebook that appears in your repository when the lab starts (one person types and one navigates, and you switch halfway). Each of you commits your own copy, names your partner at the top of the notebook, and pushes before class ends. There is no Canvas submission. Each lab is scored 2 (a real attempt, pushed by the end of class), 1 (partly done), or 0 (nothing pushed, or the notebook untouched). Labs can’t be made up; the two dropped labs cover absences. There is no lab in Week 1 (we set up codespaces together), Week 8 (the midterm), Week 15 (the agent-based model lab, graded separately), or Week 16 (presentations). Week 7’s lab is a practice midterm.

Midterm exam. The midterm is in Week 8, in one class session, and covers Modules 1–3. Part A (about 20 minutes, about 30% of the exam) is on paper with no devices: predict what code prints, find a bug, choose a method, or interpret a figure. Part B (about 50 minutes, about 70%) is one notebook in your codespace about a single economic scenario: load and summarize data, make a labeled figure, solve for an equilibrium numerically, run one optimization or simulation, and write a short interpretation. In Part B you may use the course website, your own repository, and your own notes. AI tools, messaging, and getting help from other people are not allowed. The notebook is released to your repository when Part B starts and collected when time is up, so commit and push before the end. The Week 7 lab is a full practice run in the same setup. If you have exam accommodations through the Access Center, send me your letter early so we can arrange the time you need.

Capstone project. Teams form in Week 5 and propose a question in Week 7, usually a market-structure comparison, a tax or welfare analysis, or a small general-equilibrium economy, using public data or a calibrated model. Each team checks in during Week 12 and presents in Week 16. The final submission is a team repository with the project notebook, a 1–2 page written summary, and an AI usage disclosure. The presentation includes one AI-generated error the team caught and corrected. Team members may receive different grades if their contributions differ substantially; each member submits a short contribution statement.

Final exam. There is no final exam. Capstone presentations take place in Week 16.

Attendance, late work, and make-ups

Attendance. Attendance isn’t graded directly, but in-class labs are part of your grade and are done during class. The two dropped lab scores cover illness and other short absences. For a longer absence, contact me as soon as you can.

Late problem sets. A problem set submitted up to 48 hours late loses 10% of its points for each 24 hours, or part of 24 hours, that it’s late. After 48 hours it isn’t accepted. The Canvas submission time is the official time. With a documented university-approved absence, emergency, or accommodation, contact me for an extension without penalty.

Capstone deliverables aren’t accepted late without arrangement made in advance.

Missed midterm. If you miss the midterm because of illness, an emergency, or a university-approved absence, email me before the exam if possible, and no later than 24 hours after it, to arrange a make-up, which is an alternate version of the exam. Make-ups without a documented reason aren’t given. Arrangements for religious observance follow the University Syllabus.

Missed labs can’t be made up; the two dropped lab scores cover them.

Incomplete grades. Under Academic Regulation 90, an Incomplete is possible only when circumstances beyond your control prevent you from finishing the course’s work on time. Talk to me as soon as such a situation arises.

Collaboration

Discussing ideas, approaches, and error messages with classmates is encouraged. The code and written answers you submit for problem sets must be your own: don’t copy someone else’s code or answers or share yours, and name the classmates you worked with in your AI usage note. In-class labs and the agent-based model lab may be done in pairs; the capstone is a team project.

Your repository and privacy

Your course work lives in a private GitHub repository that only you and the course staff can see. Your GitHub username doesn’t need to be your real name. Keep your repository private, and don’t post course work publicly, during or after the course, unless an assignment says it may be shared.

Classroom expectations

We are all expected to engage in courteous, respectful discussion, including on GitHub Discussions. Bring a charged device to every class. Keep phones and unrelated browser tabs away during live coding and labs; it’s hard to catch up after missing a few steps. Behavior that disrupts the learning environment may lead to a request to leave class or referral to the appropriate disciplinary process.

Artificial intelligence (AI) policy

AI use only with acknowledgment. AI assistants are allowed in this course, following the rules below, provided you disclose how you used them. You must be able to explain and defend every line you submit. The rules change as your skills grow:

Period Chat assistants (explain, debug) Inline autocomplete Agentic tools that edit and run code for you
Weeks 1–5 Allowed Off Not for problem sets
Weeks 6–13 Allowed Allowed Not for problem sets
Weeks 14–16 and capstone Allowed Allowed Allowed, documented
Midterm and in-class exams Not allowed Off Not allowed

Why the phases: early on, autocomplete writes the syntax you’re there to learn, while a chat assistant explaining an error message teaches it.

Disclosure. Every problem set ends with an AI usage note: the tool, what you asked, what you used and where, and how you checked it. “None” is a fine answer. The capstone requires the same disclosure, plus one AI error your team caught and corrected. Undisclosed AI use, or AI use that breaks the rules above, is a violation of academic integrity (see below).

Spot checks. Each week a few students, chosen at random, walk me through one cell of their submission for about three minutes. Difficulty explaining your own work is a reason for a closer look at it, not proof of a violation.

Tools and privacy. The course default is Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat, free with your WSU login. GitHub Copilot is free for verified students. You never need to pay for an AI tool. Don’t paste personal information, other students’ work, or restricted data into consumer AI tools. The course website’s Policies page has more detail.
